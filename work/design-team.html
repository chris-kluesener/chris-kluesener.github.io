Kroger · Design leadership

Staffing a design team for a business that kept changing

I was hired as the first product design leader in Kroger’s supply chain organization. Over five years I built the team, ran it, and reshaped it more than once as products were funded, paused and restarted around us. The constant wasn’t the org chart. It was putting the right designer on the right problem.

In short

  • Challenge: build a design team from almost nothing, in a part of Kroger that had never worked with product designers, while the products it supported kept changing.
  • What I did: staffed by skill, not headcount. I learned each domain first, hired for the gaps, set up a simple operating rhythm, raised the practice across the whole pillar, and moved designers whenever priorities moved.
  • Result: a team of about 7 designers embedded in product teams across forecasting, planning, replenishment, inventory visibility and end-to-end supply chain work, and a design practice the organization now planned around.

The starting point

I joined Kroger in 2021, as the company was moving to product-led ways of working. Supply chain had product managers and engineers, but product design was new. I inherited two designers and a set of domains that ran from forecasting how much to order, to tracking inventory in thousands of stores, to the warehouses and suppliers in between.

Most of the product managers had never worked with a designer. So the first job wasn’t hiring. It was learning what each domain needed from design, and showing partners what design could do.

Staffing by need, not headcount

Before opening roles, I spent my first months doing discovery on the domains themselves: the people, the processes, the systems, and where the future state was headed. That told me which skills each area needed. A domain about to rethink its whole process needed service design and facilitation. A domain building new tools from scratch needed strong interface design. A domain still defining its problems needed research and discovery.

Hiring in a competitive design market took time. I recruited through my own network, Kroger’s design community and our recruiters, and brought in designers I’d worked with before. In the meantime I filled open roles myself, and took on extra domains where I saw design could make a difference.

I kept a board like this for my team: each designer’s domain, strengths and current focus. It made staffing conversations about fit, not just availability.

Strong designers weren’t enough on their own. What varied was the environment around them: how ready each product space was to make room for design. In some areas, product managers, tech leads and business partners pulled designers into discovery from day one. In others, design was still something that happened after decisions were made. I tracked that maturity by domain, so I knew where my job was to clear a path for my designers: building trust with partners, showing what discovery could uncover, and making the case for design activities until they became part of how the team worked.

How I ran the team

With designers spread across different product teams, the risk was a group of individuals instead of a team. I kept the operating rhythm simple and consistent:

  • Weekly 1:1s with every designer, focused on their work, their career and how I could help. Status updates moved to a separate team meeting, so 1:1s stayed about the person.
  • A weekly team meeting. Sometimes new methods or shared problems, sometimes just getting to know each other.
  • Quarterly discovery planning with each designer: what we expected to learn next quarter, and what to prioritize with our research partners.
  • Fast, structured onboarding. An onboarding plan for each new hire, OKRs within their first 4–6 weeks, and a development goal for everyone.
  • Context before assignments. A shared board for each domain mapping its product teams, stakeholders and systems, so a designer could get oriented before being asked for output.

Raising the practice beyond my team

My team didn’t work alone. With the other design leaders in the pillar, I worked on the practice everyone shared:

  • A better weekly design review. I redesigned the pillar-wide review so designers framed what feedback they needed, which made critique more actionable. I ran it every week and kept adjusting the format based on retrospectives.
  • Design PODs. Based on surveys of our designers, we set up small groups that met every other week to work across domains, backgrounds and experience levels.
  • Our first on-site Design Day, which I co-led: a full day of team building, learning and design challenges for a team that had mostly only met on video.
  • Design system federator for the pillar, plus an audit of the supply chain systems people actually used, to bring them closer to the Kroger design system.

Proving design’s value

A team only stays funded if the business can see what it does. When I started, it was rare to see an OKR that mentioned users or their experience. I worked with product leaders to change that, and by 2022 every quarter had at least one objective about measuring or improving the user experience.

I also mapped the team’s work to the company’s own goals, so leaders could see design’s contribution in terms they already tracked.

Reshaping the team as the business changed

The hardest part of the job wasn’t building the team. It was keeping it pointed at the right work as the ground moved:

  • When a product was paused, I moved its designers to work that still needed them, and worked out an arrangement with another design manager to share one designer across two teams, so both got the help they needed.
  • When funding didn’t come through, we kept serving the users anyway. When a planned buyer platform wasn’t funded, the team shifted to supporting buyers through a major system rollout, and that work became the evidence that got the platform funded later.
  • When the business reorganized, so did we. In early 2025 I moved the team from a structure that mirrored product verticals to one aligned with business outcomes and workstreams.

What I learned

  • Staffing is never finished. In a business that changes, the team structure is a hypothesis. I learned to revisit it every quarter instead of defending it.
  • Get out of the way so designers can own it. As a first-time manager covering open roles, I stayed in the details longer than I should have. The team grew fastest once I delegated more and coached instead of doing.
  • Context is part of onboarding. Designers do their best work when they understand a domain’s people, history and politics before they’re asked to produce. I made that context part of every new assignment.
  • Protect time for my own growth. In busy years, my own learning was the first thing to go. Leading a team well means modeling the growth I ask of them.
